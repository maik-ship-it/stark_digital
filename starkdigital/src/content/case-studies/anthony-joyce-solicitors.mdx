A Dublin law firm had been running Google Ads for years without a reliable way to
tell what the campaigns were producing. In July 2025, with no evidence either
way, they switched the ads off.

We took the account on 25 November 2025. Ten months later it had produced 596
tracked enquiries, the cost of each one had fallen from over €100 to under €22,
and the firm had at one point increased its budget almost fivefold.

This is what we did, in the order we did it, including the month it went badly.

## What we found when we arrived

The firm had been running Google Ads since at least 2022, and kept detailed
monthly marketing reports, which it gave us access to. What those reports could
not do was connect the ad spend to the enquiries it produced. The previous
campaigns could not be measured, so nobody could say with confidence whether
they were working.

In July 2025 the firm switched the campaigns off.

The reports for the following weeks record what happened next: new enquiries
across the whole firm dropped sharply, and the reports attribute the drop to
turning the ads off. Their recommendation was to restart paid advertising.

### The problem underneath the problem

Read the reports closely and one thing comes up again and again. The firm could
not see where its enquiries were coming from.

In February 2025, litigation clients appear in the firm's lead log but not in
the tracking software, so nobody can tell whether they came from ads or from
organic search. In April and May, the reports say paid campaigns are generating
traffic but nobody can confirm whether any of it turns into client files. In
July 2025, a lapse in conversion tracking leaves nine new clients with no known
source at all.

For eighteen months the firm was paying for something it could not measure, and
so nobody could fix it. That is where we started.

We also looked at the change history on the old campaigns. Each of the five main
campaigns carries around 700 recorded status changes, almost all of them
switching the campaign on or off. That pattern is consistent with automated
scheduling rather than manual optimisation, and a campaign switched on and off
that often never accumulates enough continuous data for Google's bidding to
learn from.

## Step one: fix the measurement, not the campaigns

The first three weeks were spent on tracking, not on advertising.

We connected WhatConverts to the firm's phone system and website forms, so that
every call and every form submission could be matched to the search that
produced it. We fixed a mismatch between Google's iOS click identifiers and the
tracking software that had been silently dropping a share of conversions. We set
up the phone routing so that ad-driven calls could be told apart from everything
else.

None of this produces enquiries. It is unglamorous, and in the first month the
firm could see nothing for it. But it meant that from December onwards every
euro spent could be traced to what it produced, and every decision after that
was made on real numbers rather than on how a month felt.

The first month, November 2025, cost €643 and produced six tracked enquiries at
€107 each. That figure is in the table below, because it is the honest starting
point.

## Step two: one campaign per practice area

The old account ran broad campaigns with names like "Solicitors in Dublin" and
"No Win No Fee". A firm that handles immigration, personal injury, medical
negligence and notarial work does not have one type of client. It has four,
searching for different things, worth different amounts, converting at different
rates.

We rebuilt the account with a separate campaign for each practice area, each
with its own budget, its own keywords, its own ads and its own landing page.
This is more work to set up and more work to run, and it is the only way to find
out which parts of a law firm's advertising actually pay.

Ten months on, the answer is clear. Immigration produces enquiries at roughly a
third of the cost of medical negligence. That is not a criticism of either
campaign. It is information the firm did not have before, and it now decides
where the budget goes.

## Step three: pages built for the search, not the firm's homepage

The old campaigns sent every click to the firm's main website. Somebody who has
just searched for "citizenship application solicitor" and lands on a general law
firm homepage has to find their own way to the right page, and most do not.

We built dedicated landing pages for the immigration and notarial campaigns,
each written for the specific search that brings the visitor there, with one
clear way to get in touch and nothing else. The pages sit on the firm's own
domain, so the trust of the firm's name carries through.

The immigration page has been live for most of the engagement and takes the
majority of the account's traffic. Its click-through rate of 8.6 per cent across
the full period, rising above 15 per cent in September 2026, is several times
the account's previous average.

## Step four: the campaign nobody else would have built

Irish nurses and doctors who want to register to practise in Australia need
certain documents certified in a specific way that the Australian regulator,
AHPRA, will accept. A notary who understands exactly what AHPRA requires is hard
to find. The people looking for one are on a deadline, have few alternatives,
and search with unusual precision.

This is a tiny market.

## What happened

<PerformanceChart />

<MonthTable />

Look at the bars first. From March to July the firm more than quadrupled its
monthly budget. Nobody quadruples an advertising budget on trust. They do it
because the previous increase paid for itself and there is room for more. That
is the clearest evidence on this page that the account was working, and it is
the firm's decision rather than our claim.

Then read the last column of the table. Cost per enquiry started at €107 and finished at €22.
The improvement is not a straight line, and April is a genuinely poor month:
fewer clicks, fewer enquiries, and the worst cost per enquiry since the first
weeks. We were adjusting the personal injury campaign at the time and got the
balance wrong for about three weeks. It is in the table because a table without
a bad month is not a real table.

## The month we turned it down

July was the peak: the highest spend, and with 130 tracked enquiries the
strongest month by a distance. After it, the firm chose to scale the budget back.

So from August we brought the immigration budget down in steps, and the
account's spend fell by half. Cost per enquiry still fell to its lowest level
yet, €22 in September, because the account by then had ten months of continuous
data and Google's bidding had learned which searches convert.

## What we would say to another firm

Most of what worked here is not clever. It is the order things were done in.

Measurement first, because without it every other decision is a guess. One
campaign per practice area, because a law firm is several businesses under one
name. A page built for each search, because the firm's homepage is written for
people who already know the firm. And a willingness to turn the budget down when
the firm says it has enough, because the campaign exists to serve the firm and
not the other way round.

  followed from that.
